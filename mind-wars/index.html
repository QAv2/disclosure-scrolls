The Program

Project MKULTRA

On April 13, 1953, CIA Director Allen Dulles authorized a program to explore “the use of biological and chemical materials in altering human behavior.” It would run for 20 years across 149 sub-projects at approximately 80 institutions — universities, hospitals, prisons, military bases. Its name was MKULTRA.

The subjects were often unwitting. The methods included LSD, electroshock, sensory deprivation, hypnosis, and psychological torture. The goal was control — not understanding. Not healing. Control.

Documented
MKULTRA encompassed 149 sub-projects across approximately 80 institutions (including 44 colleges and universities, among them Harvard, Stanford, Columbia, and McGill, as well as the National Institute of Mental Health). Methods included LSD administration to unwitting subjects, electroshock, sensory deprivation, isolation, verbal and sexual abuse, and hypnosis. The program ran from 1953 to 1973.
Senate Select Committee on Intelligence, “Project MKULTRA” (August 3, 1977). Church Committee Final Report, Book I (1976). CIA admission to Congress.
Documented
In 1973, CIA Director Richard Helms ordered all MKULTRA files destroyed. The directive was carried out. Yet roughly 20,000 pages survived — misfiled in the CIA’s financial records division and uncovered in July 1977 following a FOIA request by investigative journalist John Marks and a records search ordered by Director of Central Intelligence Admiral Stansfield Turner. Those pages triggered the August 1977 joint Senate hearings (Intelligence Committee and Kennedy Health Subcommittee) and formed the foundation of Marks’s 1979 book and subsequent public disclosures.
Senate Select Committee hearings (August 3, 1977). John Marks, The Search for the “Manchurian Candidate” (1979). CIA Inspector General Report (1963, declassified).

Operation Midnight Climax

Documented
Operation Midnight Climax: The CIA operated safe houses in San Francisco and New York where sex workers were hired to lure unwitting men. The subjects were secretly dosed with LSD and other drugs, then observed and filmed through two-way mirrors by CIA agents. Federal Bureau of Narcotics officer George Hunter White ran the San Francisco safe house from 1955 to 1965 (following a 1963 CIA Inspector General recommendation to shut down the operation; the New York site closed in 1966). His personal diaries — originally held at the now-closed Foothill College Electronics Museum and now archived at Stanford University Libraries and History San Jose — document the program in detail. White later wrote: “I toiled wholeheartedly in the vineyards because it was fun, fun, fun.”
Senate Select Committee hearings (1977). Church Committee testimony. CIA Inspector General Report (1963, declassified). George H. White Papers, Stanford University Libraries (SearchWorks #4085021) & History San Jose.

The First Casualty

Documented
Frank Olson, a U.S. Army biochemist working on CIA biological weapons research, was secretly dosed with LSD by MKULTRA director Sidney Gottlieb at a retreat on November 19, 1953. Nine days later, Olson fell from the window of Room 1018A (10th floor) at the Statler Hotel in New York City. The CIA ruled it a suicide. A 1994 exhumation and forensic examination led by George Washington University forensic scientist James Starrs found a cranial injury consistent with a blow to the head before the fall. In July 1975, the Olson family received a personal apology from President Gerald Ford, followed in October 1976 by a $750,000 congressional settlement (Private Law 94-126). The case has never been resolved.

Psychic Driving

Documented

The Weapons

While MKULTRA explored chemical and psychological manipulation, a parallel track pursued something more precise: the direct transmission of information into the human brain using electromagnetic radiation. The bio-physics is peer-reviewed. The defense research was classified.

The Frey Effect

Documented
The microwave auditory effect was first reported in peer-reviewed literature in 1961 by neuroscientist Allan H. Frey in the journal Aerospace Medicine (and expanded in the Journal of Applied Physiology in 1962). Pulsed, modulated microwave radiation at specific frequencies produces sounds perceived inside the head — clicks, buzzes, hisses, and knocking — with no external speaker, no earpiece, and no physical contact. In 1973, Joseph C. Sharp and H. Mark Grove at the Walter Reed Army Institute of Research extended the effect to transmit intelligible spoken words (the digits 1 through 10). The underlying thermoelastic effect has been independently replicated in laboratories worldwide for over six decades.
Allan H. Frey, “Auditory System Response to Radio Frequency Energy,” Aerospace Medicine 32 (1961), PMID 13895080. Frey, Journal of Applied Physiology 17 (1962). Don R. Justesen, American Psychologist 30 (1975) (reporting Sharp & Grove, 1973). Replication: Foster & Finch (1974), Elder & Chou (2003), Lin & Wang (2007).

The Moscow Signal

Documented
From the 1950s through 1976, the Soviet Union continuously irradiated the U.S. Embassy in Moscow with low-level microwave radiation. Ambassador Walter Stoessel suffered bleeding eyes and a blood disorder during his Moscow tenure and later died of leukemia in 1986; two earlier ambassadors, Charles Bohlen and Llewellyn Thompson, also died of cancer. The State Department kept the irradiation secret from embassy staff for years and commissioned an epidemiological study by Johns Hopkins University led by Dr. Abraham Lilienfeld. Published unclassified in 1978, the Lilienfeld study officially found no statistically significant increase in overall mortality or cancer rates compared to control Eastern European posts — a conclusion embassy personnel and outside critics disputed over methodology, follow-up duration, and incomplete cancer-incidence tracking.
Abraham M. Lilienfeld et al., Foreign Service Health Status Study (Johns Hopkins University / U.S. Dept. of State, 1978). J. Mark Elwood, “Microwaves in the Cold War: The Moscow Embassy Study and Its Interpretation,” Int. J. Epidemiol. (2012, PMC3509929) · PubMed 23151144. Declassified State Department cables. AP and UPI reporting (1976).
Documented

DARPA’s interest in directed energy did not end with Pandora — it evolved.

Havana Syndrome

Documented
Beginning in late 2016, U.S. and Canadian diplomats in Havana, Cuba reported sudden onset of cognitive difficulties, hearing loss, vertigo, nausea, and neurological injuries with no apparent external cause. By 2024, similar Anomalous Health Incidents (AHIs) were reported at U.S. facilities in China, Austria, Colombia, India, Vietnam, and Washington, D.C. — affecting an estimated 1,500+ U.S. government personnel. Early neuroimaging studies published in JAMA (2018, 2019) reported measurable white matter differences in affected diplomats; however, two five-year NIH Intramural studies published in JAMA in March 2024 found no significant MRI-detectable brain injury or biomarker differences between AHI patients and matched healthy controls, while affirming that patients’ symptoms were real and disabling.
Swanson et al., JAMA (2018, PMID 29450484). Verma et al., JAMA (2019, PMID 31334794). Chan et al., JAMA (2024, PMID 38497797). Pierpaoli et al., JAMA (2024, PMID 38497800). 60 Minutes investigations (2022, 2024).
Documented
In its December 2020 expert committee assessment, the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine concluded that “directed, pulsed radiofrequency energy appears to be the most plausible mechanism” for the Havana Syndrome injuries — consistent with the Frey Effect mechanism documented since 1961. While the committee’s published finding is on the public record, the underlying causal mechanism remained sharply contested across intelligence and medical reviews from 2023 through 2026.

Act IV

The Suppression

In 1995, the CIA commissioned an external review of the Stargate program by the American Institutes for Research. The review acknowledged statistically significant results — then recommended termination anyway, citing lack of “actionable intelligence.” The program was declared dead. Former program officers later stated the work continued under different names and different budgets.

Documented
The 1995 AIR review of Stargate (An Evaluation of Remote Viewing: Research and Applications, Mumford, Rose, & Goslin, Sept. 29, 1995), with evaluation papers by statistician Jessica Utts (UC Davis) and psychologist and skeptic Ray Hyman (University of Oregon), reached split conclusions. Utts wrote: “Using the standards applied to any other area of science, it is concluded that psychic functioning has been well established. The statistical results of the studies examined are far beyond what is expected by chance.” Hyman agreed the results were statistically anomalous but questioned the methodology, the lack of independent third-party replication, and the absence of a theoretical mechanism. The program was terminated regardless.
Jessica Utts, “An Assessment of the Evidence for Psychic Functioning” (AIR, 1995) · Utts response to Ray Hyman. Mumford, Rose, & Goslin, An Evaluation of Remote Viewing: Research and Applications (American Institutes for Research, 1995). Congressional appropriations records.

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""
Extract files hidden in a CNTYSEED steganographic image.
Only requires: pip install Pillow
Usage: python3 extract.py <image.png> <output_dir>
"""
import hashlib, io, os, struct, sys, tarfile, zlib
from PIL import Image

MAGIC = b"CNTYSEED"
HDR_SIZE = 64
HDR_FMT = "!8sBBBBII32sHH8s"


def lsb_decode(flat, depth, num_bytes):
    total_bits = num_bytes * 8
    pad = depth - (total_bits % depth) if total_bits % depth else 0
    slots = (total_bits + pad) // depth
    mask = (1 << depth) - 1
    bits = []
    for i in range(slots):
        val = flat[i] & mask
        for shift in range(depth - 1, -1, -1):
            bits.append((val >> shift) & 1)
    out = bytearray()
    for i in range(0, num_bytes * 8, 8):
        byte = 0
        for b in range(8):
            byte = (byte << 1) | bits[i + b]
        out.append(byte)
    return bytes(out)


def extract(image_path, output_dir):
    img = Image.open(image_path).convert("RGB")
    flat = [ch for px in img.getdata() for ch in px]

    hdr = None
    for depth in (1, 2, 3, 4):
        raw = lsb_decode(flat, depth, HDR_SIZE)
        if raw[:8] != MAGIC:
            continue
        fields = struct.unpack(HDR_FMT, raw)
        if fields[3] == depth:
            hdr = {
                "flags": fields[2], "lsb_depth": depth,
                "compression": fields[4], "original_size": fields[5],
                "compressed_size": fields[6], "sha256": fields[7],
            }
            break

    if not hdr:
        print("No embedded data found.")
        sys.exit(1)

    if hdr["flags"] & 0x01:
        print("This image is encrypted.")
        sys.exit(1)

    print(f"Found: {hdr['original_size']:,} bytes, {hdr['lsb_depth']}-LSB")

    total = HDR_SIZE + hdr["compressed_size"]
    raw = lsb_decode(flat, hdr["lsb_depth"], total)
    payload = raw[HDR_SIZE:]

    if hdr["compression"] == 1:
        payload = zlib.decompress(payload)
    elif hdr["compression"] == 2:
        import lzma
        payload = lzma.decompress(payload)

    if hashlib.sha256(payload).digest() == hdr["sha256"]:
        print("Integrity: VERIFIED")
    else:
        print("WARNING: SHA-256 mismatch")

    os.makedirs(output_dir, exist_ok=True)
    if hdr["flags"] & 0x02:
        with tarfile.open(fileobj=io.BytesIO(payload), mode="r") as tar:
            tar.extractall(path=output_dir, filter="data")
        print(f"Extracted to: {output_dir}/")
    else:
        with open(os.path.join(output_dir, "extracted"), "wb") as f:
            f.write(payload)
        print(f"Written to: {output_dir}/extracted")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    if len(sys.argv) != 3:
        print(f"Usage: {sys.argv[0]} <image.png> <output_dir>")
        sys.exit(1)
    extract(sys.argv[1], sys.argv[2])
