The History

Weather modification is not theoretical. Governments have been doing it since 1946. Here are the programs they don’t teach in school.

The Programs

Vincent Schaefer
General Electric (GE) researcher — first cloud-seeding flight, 1946
USAF

Documented
Project Cirrus (1947–1952): Following General Electric (GE) researcher Vincent Schaefer’s November 1946 cloud-seeding flight, GE, the Army Signal Corps, Office of Naval Research, and U.S. Air Force jointly backed Project Cirrus. In October 1947, ~180 pounds (~82 kg) of dry ice was dropped into a hurricane — which reversed course and struck Georgia, causing $2 million in damage. Project Stormfury (1962–1983): U.S. Weather Bureau / ESSA / National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) and U.S. Navy program to weaken hurricanes with silver iodide. 21 years of operation. Hurricane Debbie (1969) saw a 31% wind speed reduction. Project Cumulus (UK, 1949–1952): Royal Air Force (RAF) cloud seeding over England. On August 15–16, 1952, 230mm of rain fell on Lynmouth in 24 hours, killing 34 people. Subsequent meteorological analyses attributed the 1947 hurricane turn, Debbie’s wind drop (natural eyewall replacement), and the 1952 Lynmouth storm to natural weather dynamics rather than seeding — but the files prove governments were actively trying.
Earth Magazine, “Benchmarks: Disaster of Project Cirrus” · NOAA AOML, Project Stormfury · The Guardian / BBC Radio 4 (2001), Project Cumulus.

Two countries. Four decades. Hurricanes seeded, clouds bombed with dry ice and silver iodide, trials kept classified until journalists and FOIA pried them open. And that was before the Pentagon took it to war.

Operation Popeye (1967–1972)

This is the one that changes everything.

Documented

The ENMOD Treaty (1977)

Documented
The Convention on the Prohibition of Military or Any Other Hostile Use of Environmental Modification Techniques. 78 state parties. The U.S. ratified in 1980. Bans weather modification for hostile purposes with “widespread, long-lasting or severe effects.” Does NOT address non-hostile uses. Does NOT require disclosure. Does NOT cover “climate remediation.” The treaty banned the weapon. It said nothing about everything else.
UN Office for Disarmament Affairs — ENMOD Convention · UNODA Treaty Status (78 State Parties).

The treaty addressed the war. It left the sky wide open.

The Patents

A defense contractor sat down and designed a system to spray aluminum oxide in the stratosphere. Then they patented it. Then Raytheon bought the company’s defense unit and inherited the patent portfolio.

Patents don’t prove deployment. But they prove intent, capability, and corporate lineage.

The Welsbach Patent (1991)

Hughes Aircraft
Filed the patent — acquired by Raytheon (1997)
Raytheon
Acquired Hughes defense unit (1997); patent expired 2010
Documented
US Patent 5,003,186 — “Stratospheric Welsbach Seeding for Reduction of Global Warming.” Proposes dispersing particles of aluminum oxide and thorium oxide into the stratosphere at 7–13 km altitude to convert trapped infrared radiation. Particle size: 10–100 microns. One proposed delivery method: “via the jet fuel.” Particles “may remain in suspension for up to one year.”
USPTO / Google Patents — US 5,003,186. Inventors: David B. Chang, I-Fu Shih. Filed April 23, 1990; granted March 26, 1991; expired 2010.

Was it deployed? No evidence. The patent expired in 2010 without documented test flights or government contracts, and its citation chain consists mostly of later conceptual geoengineering filings rather than operational defense systems. The inventors were infrared (IR) and optics engineers, not atmospheric scientists. This is not the footprint of an active deployment program.

But it exists. A defense contractor patented stratospheric metal-oxide spraying, and Raytheon inherited that portfolio when it acquired Hughes’s defense business in 1997. The patent has since expired into the public domain — and the full filing remains publicly verifiable in USPTO records.

Bernard Eastlund and the HAARP Patent

Bernard J. Eastlund
Physicist — HAARP patent inventor
HAARP
3.6 MW ionospheric research facility, Alaska

Bernard J. Eastlund was a physicist working for ARCO — the Atlantic Richfield Company, an oil giant. In 1985, he filed a patent through ARCO’s subsidiary ARCO Power Technologies, Inc. (APTI) for a method of altering the ionosphere using a ground-based antenna array — later linked in public debate to the High-Frequency Active Auroral Research Program (HAARP). The listed applications included disrupting missiles, interfering with communications, and altering upper-atmosphere wind patterns. Eastlund died in 2007. His patent lived on — traveling through a chain of defense contractors before expiring into the public domain in 2005.

Documented
US Patent 4,686,605 — The “HAARP patent.” The patent specifies 1–100 GW of power. HAARP operates at ~3.6 MW — roughly 300 to 30,000 times less. HAARP program manager John Heckscher stated: “HAARP certainly does not have anything to do with Eastlund’s thing.” Whether or not HAARP implements Eastlund’s vision, the patent chain tells its own story.
USPTO / Google Patents — US 4,686,605 (filed Jan. 10, 1985; granted Aug. 11, 1987; APTI, Inc., later acquired via E-Systems and Raytheon by BAE Systems; expired 2005) · University of Alaska Fairbanks — HAARP FAQ.

The Landscape

Eastlund and Welsbach aren’t alone. A 2014 academic study identified 143 patent families (910 members worldwide) related to climate engineering. The U.S. government itself holds a 2021 patent for electrostatic cloud seeding (US 11,116,150, Secretary of Agriculture). Eastman Kodak filed a solar-geoengineering patent application in 2022 for dispersing porous polymer particles in the stratosphere (EP 4,301,126, published January 2024). Even NASA patented a 1974 sounding-rocket system for releasing barium ion clouds in the upper atmosphere to trace geomagnetic field lines (US 3,813,875) — a legitimate space-physics instrument frequently misquoted online as weather modification. Likewise, the widely cited August 1996 Air University paper Weather as a Force Multiplier: Owning the Weather in 2025 (Col. Tamzy J. House et al.) was a speculative student-and-faculty future-studies exercise carrying an explicit disclaimer that it did not represent official U.S. Air Force policy.

Documented

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""
Extract files hidden in a CNTYSEED steganographic image.
Only requires: pip install Pillow
Usage: python3 extract.py <image.png> <output_dir>
"""
import hashlib, io, os, struct, sys, tarfile, zlib
from PIL import Image

MAGIC = b"CNTYSEED"
HDR_SIZE = 64
HDR_FMT = "!8sBBBBII32sHH8s"


def lsb_decode(flat, depth, num_bytes):
    total_bits = num_bytes * 8
    pad = depth - (total_bits % depth) if total_bits % depth else 0
    slots = (total_bits + pad) // depth
    mask = (1 << depth) - 1
    bits = []
    for i in range(slots):
        val = flat[i] & mask
        for shift in range(depth - 1, -1, -1):
            bits.append((val >> shift) & 1)
    out = bytearray()
    for i in range(0, num_bytes * 8, 8):
        byte = 0
        for b in range(8):
            byte = (byte << 1) | bits[i + b]
        out.append(byte)
    return bytes(out)


def extract(image_path, output_dir):
    img = Image.open(image_path).convert("RGB")
    flat = [ch for px in img.getdata() for ch in px]

    hdr = None
    for depth in (1, 2, 3, 4):
        raw = lsb_decode(flat, depth, HDR_SIZE)
        if raw[:8] != MAGIC:
            continue
        fields = struct.unpack(HDR_FMT, raw)
        if fields[3] == depth:
            hdr = {
                "flags": fields[2], "lsb_depth": depth,
                "compression": fields[4], "original_size": fields[5],
                "compressed_size": fields[6], "sha256": fields[7],
            }
            break

    if not hdr:
        print("No embedded data found.")
        sys.exit(1)

    if hdr["flags"] & 0x01:
        print("This image is encrypted.")
        sys.exit(1)

    print(f"Found: {hdr['original_size']:,} bytes, {hdr['lsb_depth']}-LSB")

    total = HDR_SIZE + hdr["compressed_size"]
    raw = lsb_decode(flat, hdr["lsb_depth"], total)
    payload = raw[HDR_SIZE:]

    if hdr["compression"] == 1:
        payload = zlib.decompress(payload)
    elif hdr["compression"] == 2:
        import lzma
        payload = lzma.decompress(payload)

    if hashlib.sha256(payload).digest() == hdr["sha256"]:
        print("Integrity: VERIFIED")
    else:
        print("WARNING: SHA-256 mismatch")

    os.makedirs(output_dir, exist_ok=True)
    if hdr["flags"] & 0x02:
        with tarfile.open(fileobj=io.BytesIO(payload), mode="r") as tar:
            tar.extractall(path=output_dir, filter="data")
        print(f"Extracted to: {output_dir}/")
    else:
        with open(os.path.join(output_dir, "extracted"), "wb") as f:
            f.write(payload)
        print(f"Written to: {output_dir}/extracted")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    if len(sys.argv) != 3:
        print(f"Usage: {sys.argv[0]} <image.png> <output_dir>")
        sys.exit(1)
    extract(sys.argv[1], sys.argv[2])
